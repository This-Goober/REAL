# title: How you can start loving practicing with a drone [Part 1: sound]
# assets: "Reel Vids/Drone_Part 1"

[broll pool: pag(pov, office sitting), bach(pov, office standing), prokofiev(phone, piano), debussy(phone, piano, diff lighting), scales(mirror, office standing) — ~30s each, cuts mid-word]

So this is a compilation of me practicing some of my recent repertoire throughout the
week. Obviously enough, it's very noticeable that there is this obnoxious buzzing sound
in the background at all times. That sound is called a drone. [text: "Drone."] You might
wonder why the hell some people like me voluntarily choose to inflict this aural
suffering [img: young-schizophrenic-man-ears.webp] both on my family and myself. But
I'd like to explain why I use it so heavily, and why you also should consider trying it
too. [black] [text: "FYI, you might need some basic understanding of sinusoidal functions and sound waves to fully understand the rest of this video"]

---

Contrary to what people might think, its helpfulness doesn't have to do with manually
comparing my pitch to the pitch of the drone mentally — in fact that's what I'm trying
to avoid. The reason, rather, has more to do with sound quality: despite being like a
deafening laser, this particular synthetic soundwave option on the app
[img: tunable-screenshot] Tunable [img: tunable-icon] is most similar to that of
soundwaves produced by string instruments. Because the soundwaves have similar nature,
they will more directly [img: two-soundwaves, stage 1] interact and collide with each
other, producing a more obvious [reveal stage 2: third-soundwave] third soundwave,
which has its own frequency and pitch.

---

That third soundwave is called an overtone. Overtones are actually audible to us if you
listen carefully, and its pitch and quality will change depending on its parent
soundwave frequencies — aka us [img: q6hz57cbg4611.jpg] and
the drone [img: shopping.webp]. On the spectrum of all possible pitches
to combine, including semitones [img: piano-slider-metaphor], most are dissonant with
each other and have soundwaves that collide a lot when placed on top of each other —
and we're talking about a collision every millionth of a second. As a result, the
overtone that is produced by them has soundwaves that look like this
[img: dissonant-overtone-wave] and as you can perhaps intuitively infer from the shape,
they kinda sound like "wawawawa" [text: "wawawa"] noise in the background.

[clip: me demonstrating dissonant wawawa with drone, for 4s]

---

However, like with any rule, there are exceptions. There's a very specific set of
intervals where the overtone's frequency is an integer multiple of the parent
frequencies, meaning the overtone's wave fits synergetically with the parent waves
rather than constantly colliding with them. These instances are universally referred to
as perfect intervals. [img: music-intervals] The frequency of soundwaves
in fifths have a 3:2 ratio, fourths have a 4:3 ratio, and octaves have a 2:1 ratio.
[img: ratio-diagrams] So the overtone's wave they produce looks more like this
[img: consonant-overtone-wave], which has a more diffused rather than distinct
[img again: dissonant-overtone-wave] amplitude fluctuation, thus giving them their
characteristic "perfectly" stable and pure sound.

[clip: me demonstrating perfect-interval purity with drone, for 4s]

---

The reason why this makes having a drone on helpful for training intonation is because
since the intervals are perfect, your intonation also has to be perfect
[suggestion: "face palm — why didn't I think of that" meme] in order to produce the
clear sound. Even being microscopically out of tune will produce the wacky dissonant
overtones instead, indicating immediately to you that you have something to work on.
Because I admittedly only have relative rather than perfect pitch, instead of just
"listening harder" I'd 1000% prefer saving that mental energy and just react to wiggly
sounds in the air. [broll: me practicing, putting my bow down]

[black]

So yeah. [text: "practice with a drone"] [sfx: drop]
